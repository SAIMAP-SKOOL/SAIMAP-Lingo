# Level C1 · Precision and style

At this level the grammar is largely known. The work now is choice: saying the same thing in several ways and knowing which one fits the moment. From here on, the *Grammar* section is called *Language*, and the *Sounds & Spelling* box gives way to notes on register and usage.

## Unit 38 · Register and tone

**Goals:** adapt your language to the situation, move between formal and informal, soften and hedge. **Language:** formal and informal equivalents, nominalisation, hedging, politeness strategies.

![](u38.png)

### Text

Compare three ways of saying the same thing.

*To a friend:* "Sorry, can't make it tonight. Something's come up at work. Fancy doing it next week instead?"

*To a colleague:* "I'm afraid I won't be able to come to the meeting this evening, as something urgent has come up. Would it be possible to rearrange it for next week?"

*To a client:* "I regret to inform you that, owing to unforeseen circumstances, I shall be unable to attend this evening's meeting. I would be most grateful if we could reschedule at your earliest convenience."

Nothing has changed except the relationship. The first message is short, leaves out the subject and uses a phrasal verb. The third is long, replaces verbs with nouns and puts as much distance as possible between the writer and the bad news. A fluent speaker moves along this scale without thinking. A learner often has only the middle of it.

| Informal | Neutral | Formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kids | children | offspring, minors |
| get | obtain, receive | acquire, be in receipt of |
| ask for | request | solicit |
| need | require | necessitate |
| start | begin | commence |
| end | finish | conclude, terminate |
| buy | purchase | procure |
| help | assist | facilitate |
| about | concerning | with regard to |
| so | therefore | consequently, hence |
| but | however | nevertheless, notwithstanding |
| sorry | I apologise | I regret |
| thanks | thank you | I am most grateful |
| a lot of | many, much | a great deal of, numerous |
| fancy (doing) | would like (to do) | wish (to do) |

### Language

**1. What makes a text formal?**

| Feature | Informal | Formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contractions | I can't | I cannot |
| Vocabulary | phrasal verbs, short words | single verbs of Latin origin |
| Subject | often omitted (*Can't make it*) | always present |
| Voice | active, personal | passive, impersonal |
| Structure | verbs | nouns (nominalisation) |
| Directness | direct | hedged, distanced |

**2. Nominalisation**

Formal English turns verbs and adjectives into nouns.

*We **decided** to close the branch because sales **fell**.* → *The **decision** to close the branch followed a **fall** in sales.*
*People were **angry** because prices **rose**.* → *The **rise** in prices caused widespread **anger**.*

**3. Hedging**

To avoid sounding too certain or too blunt: *It **seems / appears** that… · This **may** suggest… · There is **some** evidence that… · It **could be argued** that… · **To some extent** · **On the whole** · **Arguably***

**4. Politeness and distance**

The further a form moves from the present and the direct, the more polite it sounds.

*Can you help me? → Could you help me? → I wonder if you could help me. → I was wondering whether you might be able to help me.*

Softeners: *I'm afraid… · Actually… · If you don't mind… · Would you mind if I… · I don't suppose you could…*

**5. British understatement**

*not bad* (= good) · *quite good* (= fairly good, not very) · *a bit of a problem* (= a serious problem) · *I'm not too keen* (= I dislike it) · *with respect* (= I disagree)

### Watch out!

- *Quite* is weaker in British English than in American English: *quite good* is moderate praise. With extreme adjectives it means «completamente»: *quite impossible*.
- Words of Latin origin feel natural to a Spanish speaker but sound formal in English. *Commence, purchase, terminate* in a chat with friends are comic. Use *start, buy, end*.
- *Shall* with *I* and *we* is formal or old-fashioned in statements, but normal in offers: *Shall I…?*
- Do not mix registers: «I regret to inform you that I can't make it» jars.
- *Please* at the start of an order does not make it a request. *Please send me the file* is still an order. *Could you send me the file?* is a request.

a) Sorry I didn't write back sooner. b) We need more info about the kids. c) Thanks a lot for helping us out. d) The meeting's been put off.

**2.** Nominalise the words in italics.
a) The company *grew* rapidly, which surprised investors. b) She *refused* to comment, and this made things worse. c) Unemployment *increased* sharply last year.

**3.** Make these more tentative or polite.
a) This proves that the policy failed. b) Send me the report by Friday. c) You are wrong. d) I want to leave early.

### Answers

**1.** (examples) a) I apologise for the delay in replying. b) We require further information concerning the children. c) I am most grateful for your assistance. d) The meeting has been postponed. **2.** a) The company's rapid growth surprised investors. b) Her refusal to comment made things worse. c) There was a sharp increase in unemployment last year. **3.** (examples) a) This would seem to suggest that the policy was not entirely successful. b) Would it be possible to send me the report by Friday? c) I'm not sure I entirely agree. d) I was wondering whether I might leave a little early.

## Unit 39 · Emphasis

**Goals:** highlight information, correct and contrast, write with rhetorical force. **Language:** cleft sentences, inversion after negative adverbials, fronting, emphatic *do*.

![](u39.png)

### Text

Never have I been so wrong about a place. What I expected from Glasgow was grey skies and grim streets. What I found was the friendliest city I have ever visited.

It was the people who made the difference. No sooner had I got off the train than a stranger offered to carry my bag. Not only did she walk me to my hotel, but she also drew me a map of her favourite cafés. Rarely does that happen in a capital city.

The architecture I had read about; the humour I had not. Glaswegians do love a joke, and it is usually at their own expense. Only when I tried to understand a taxi driver did I realise how much English I still had to learn. Little did I know that he was speaking slowly for my benefit.

All I can say is this: go. Under no circumstances should you leave Scotland without seeing it. So warm was the welcome that I have been back three times.

| English | Spanish |
| --- | --- |
| grim | sombrío, deprimente |
| stranger | desconocido |
| at someone's expense | a costa de alguien |
| for my benefit | por mí, para ayudarme |
| hardly / scarcely / barely | apenas |
| seldom / rarely | rara vez |
| under no circumstances | bajo ningún concepto |
| on no account | de ningún modo |
| at no time | en ningún momento |
| not until | no… hasta |
| no sooner … than | apenas… cuando |
| little did I know | no tenía ni idea de que |
| what matters is | lo que importa es |
| the thing is | lo que pasa es que |
| indeed | en efecto, de hecho |

### Language

**1. Cleft sentences with *it***

*It is / was* + the highlighted element + *that / who*.

Neutral: *The people made the difference.* → ***It was the people who** made the difference.* · ***It was in Glasgow that** I learnt this. · **It wasn't until** midnight **that** we arrived.*

**2. Cleft sentences with *what***

***What** I expected **was** grey skies. · **What** surprised me **was** the humour. · **What** she did **was** (to) draw me a map. · **All** I can say **is** this. · **The thing that** annoys me **is**…*

**3. Inversion after negative and restrictive adverbials**

When one of these expressions begins the sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject, as in a question. It is formal and emphatic.

| Adverbial | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Never / Rarely / Seldom | **Never have I been** so wrong. |
| Not only … but also | **Not only did she walk** me to my hotel, but she also… |
| No sooner … than | **No sooner had I got** off the train than… |
| Hardly / Scarcely … when | **Hardly had we sat** down when the phone rang. |
| Only when / Only after / Not until | **Only when** I tried… **did I realise**… |
| Under no circumstances / On no account | **Under no circumstances should you leave**. |
| Little | **Little did I know**… |

With *only when* and *not until*, the inversion is in the main clause, not in the clause that follows the adverbial.

**4. Other inversions and fronting**

***So** warm **was the welcome** that… · **Such was** the noise that… · **Had I known** (= if I had known)… · **Should you need** (= if you need) help… · **Were I** (= if I were) in your position…*

Fronting an object for contrast: ***The architecture** I had read about; **the humour** I had not.*

**5. Emphatic *do***

*Glaswegians **do** love a joke. · I **did** tell you! · **Do** sit down.*

### Watch out!

- Inversion needs an auxiliary: *Never **have I seen*** / *Rarely **does it happen***, not «Never I have seen» or «Rarely happens it».
- In a *what*-cleft the verb *be* agrees loosely; *What I need **is** two more days* is standard.
- Spanish emphasises by moving words freely («Eso lo dije yo»). English word order is fixed, so it needs these structures: ***It was I / me who** said that*.
- «Lo que» = *what*, never «that what» or «the what».
- Use inversion sparingly. One in a paragraph is powerful; five sound like a parody.

### Exercises

**1.** Rewrite to emphasise the words in italics, using an *it*-cleft.
a) *Tom* broke the window. b) I met her *in Paris*. c) We didn't leave *until six*.

**2.** Rewrite with a *what*-cleft.
a) I need a holiday. b) His attitude annoys me. c) She phoned the police.

**3.** Rewrite beginning with the words given.
a) I have never heard such nonsense. Never ___. b) She speaks French and she also speaks Russian. Not only ___. c) I had just closed my eyes when the alarm went off. No sooner ___. d) You must not open this door. On no account ___. e) If I had known, I would have come. Had ___.

### Answers

**1.** a) It was Tom who broke the window. b) It was in Paris that I met her. c) It wasn't until six that we left. **2.** a) What I need is a holiday. b) What annoys me is his attitude. c) What she did was phone the police. **3.** a) Never have I heard such nonsense. b) Not only does she speak French, but she also speaks Russian. c) No sooner had I closed my eyes than the alarm went off. d) On no account must you open this door. e) Had I known, I would have come.

## Unit 40 · Hypothetical meaning

**Goals:** express unreal situations, preferences, wishes and demands with nuance. **Language:** the unreal past, the subjunctive in English, *it's high time*, *suppose*, *as if*, formal conditionals.

![](u40.png)

### Text

Suppose you were given the power to change one law. What would it be?

I put the question to a group of sixth-formers. One girl said it was high time the voting age was lowered. "People talk about us as though we were children," she said. "I'd sooner they asked us what we think." A boy beside her insisted that every school be required to teach cooking. "If only someone had shown me how to make a proper meal," he said, "I wouldn't be living on toast."

Their teacher recommended that the debate continue the following week. She would rather the class had prepared, she admitted, but it is essential that young people be heard.

Were I to answer my own question, I would say this: but for a handful of good teachers, I should never have learnt to ask questions at all. Should you ever find yourself in a classroom, remember that.

| lower / raise | bajar / subir |
| the voting age | la edad para votar |
| require | exigir |
| demand | exigir |
| insist | insistir |
| propose | proponer |
| essential / vital | esencial / vital |
| I'd sooner | preferiría |

| but for | de no ser por |
| a handful of | un puñado de |
| as though | como si |

### Language

**1. The unreal past**

A past form after certain expressions refers to an unreal present or future, not to past time.

| Expression | Example |
| --- | --- |
| wish / if only | I wish I **knew**. If only I **had** more time. |
| would rather / would sooner + subject | I'd sooner they **asked** us. |
| it's (high) time | It's high time the age **was** lowered. |
| suppose / supposing / what if | Suppose you **were given** the power… |
| as if / as though | …as though we **were** children. |

For an unreal past, use the past perfect: *If only someone **had shown** me. · She would rather the class **had prepared**.*

**2. The subjunctive**

English has a small subjunctive: the base form of the verb for all persons, with no *-s* and no *do*. It appears in formal language after verbs and adjectives of demand and necessity.

*He insisted that every school **be** required… · She recommended that the debate **continue**. · It is essential that young people **be** heard. · They demanded that he **not leave**.*

Triggers: *demand, insist, propose, recommend, request, suggest · essential, vital, important, necessary*

In everyday British English, *should* + verb or an ordinary tense is more common: *She recommended that the debate **should continue***.

Fixed expressions: *God **save** the King · Long **live**… · **be** that as it may · **come** what may · so **be** it · if need **be** · suffice it to say*

**3. Formal conditionals**

| Neutral | Formal |
| --- | --- |
| If you ever find… | **Should you** ever find… |
| If I answered… | **Were I to** answer… |
| If I had known… | **Had I** known… |
| If it hadn't been for teachers… | **But for** teachers… / **Had it not been for**… |

**4. *were to***

Makes a future condition more remote: *If the government **were to** fall, there would be an election.*

### Watch out!

- Spanish speakers have the subjunctive instinct, which helps here; but the English form is the bare verb: *I suggest that he **go***, not «that he goes» (in formal style) and never «that he went» for the present.
- «Ya es hora de que te vayas» = *It's time you **went***, not «It's time that you go».
- «Preferiría que vinieras mañana» = *I'd rather you **came** tomorrow*.
- «Ojalá» has three translations: *I wish I **knew*** (present), *I wish I **had known*** (past), *I **hope** he comes* (real possibility).
- *I wish* + *would* cannot refer to yourself: *I wish I **could** stop*, not «I wish I would stop».

### Exercises

**1.** Complete with the correct form.
a) It's high time you ___ (tidy) your room. b) I'd rather you ___ (not tell) anyone yesterday. c) He talks as though he ___ (own) the place. d) Suppose you ___ (lose) your job. What would you do?

**2.** Rewrite using the subjunctive.
a) "You must leave at once," he demanded. He demanded that she ___. b) It is vital for everyone to be informed. It is vital that everyone ___. c) "Why doesn't he apply?" I suggested. I suggested that he ___.

**3.** Rewrite in a formal style, beginning with the word given.
a) If you need any help, please call. Should ___. b) If he resigned, there would be a crisis. Were ___. c) If it hadn't been for you, I would have failed. But ___.

### Answers

**1.** a) tidied b) hadn't told c) owned d) lost. **2.** a) leave at once b) be informed c) apply. **3.** a) Should you need any help, please call. b) Were he to resign, there would be a crisis. c) But for you, I would have failed.

## Unit 41 · Cohesion

**Goals:** write texts that flow, avoid repetition, guide the reader. **Language:** reference and substitution, ellipsis, participle clauses, discourse markers.

![](u41.png)

### Text

Having spent twenty years in journalism, I thought I knew how to write. Then I was asked to edit a colleague's report. It was accurate. It was thorough. It was also unreadable.

The problem was not the facts but the way they were joined, or rather not joined. Each sentence stood alone, repeating the nouns of the one before. I rewrote the opening paragraph, replacing the repetitions with pronouns and linking the ideas. This done, the text was a third shorter and twice as clear.

Good writers do so instinctively. They refer back and point forward. They leave out what the reader can supply and, where necessary, say so. Asked how he did it, one novelist replied that he simply imagined a friend sitting opposite, looking bored. If the friend would have interrupted, he cut the sentence. If not, he kept it.

As far as style is concerned, that is the only rule I know. The former half of my career taught me to collect facts; the latter, to connect them.

| edit | corregir, editar |
| proofread | revisar (erratas) |
| paragraph | párrafo |
| topic sentence | frase temática |
| the former / the latter | el primero / el segundo |
| the above / the following | lo anterior / lo siguiente |
| as regards / regarding | en cuanto a |
| as far as … is concerned | por lo que respecta a |
| in other words | en otras palabras |
| or rather | o mejor dicho |
| that is to say | es decir |
| furthermore / moreover | además |

### Language

**1. Reference**

*this, that, these, those, it, they, such, the former, the latter, the above, one, ones*

*Prices rose sharply. **This** led to protests.* (*this* = the whole idea) · *There are two options: a loan or a grant. **The former** must be repaid; **the latter** need not.*

**2. Substitution**

| Replace | With | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a noun | one / ones | I prefer the red **ones**. |
| a verb phrase | do / do so | Good writers **do so** instinctively. |
| a clause | so / not | "Is it true?" "I think **so**." / "I hope **not**." / "If **so**… If **not**…" |

**3. Ellipsis**

Leave out words that can be understood.

*She can speak French, and he can [speak French] too. · I'd love to [come]. · They were asked to leave but refused [to leave]. · Each sentence stood alone, [and it was] repeating the nouns.*

**4. Participle clauses**

They replace a full clause when the subject is the same. They are typical of written English.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *-ing* | at the same time; because | I rewrote it, **replacing** the repetitions. **Being** tired, I left. |
| *Having* + participle | after; because (earlier) | **Having spent** twenty years in journalism, I thought… |
| past participle | passive | **Asked** how he did it, he replied… |
| noun + participle | absolute clause | **This done**, the text was shorter. **Weather permitting**, we'll go. |

**5. Discourse markers**

| Function | Markers |
| --- | --- |
| Adding | furthermore, moreover, what is more, in addition |
| Contrasting | however, nevertheless, on the contrary, by contrast |
| Clarifying | in other words, that is to say, or rather, namely |
| Focusing | as regards, with regard to, as far as … is concerned |
| Summarising | in short, to sum up, on the whole, all in all |
| In speech | well, anyway, mind you, I mean, actually, as I was saying |

### Watch out!

- A participle clause must share the subject of the main clause. «Walking down the street, a dog bit me» says the dog was walking: *Walking down the street, **I** was bitten by a dog*.
- *On the contrary* rejects what was just said («al contrario»). *On the other hand* adds a different side («por otro lado»). They are not interchangeable.
- *In fact* and *actually* correct or reinforce; they do not mean «actualmente».
- «Creo que sí / que no» = *I think **so*** / *I don't think so*, not «I think yes».
- English tolerates far less repetition of the noun than a learner expects, but it does not drop the subject pronoun: ***It** was accurate*, never «Was accurate».

a) I've lost my pen. Can I borrow your pen? b) "Will it rain?" "I hope it won't rain." c) She said she would call, and she called. d) We can take the train or the coach. The train is faster, but the coach is cheaper.

### Answers

**1.** a) Can I borrow yours? b) I hope not. c) …and she did. d) The former is faster, but the latter is cheaper. **2.** a) Not knowing the way, I asked a policeman. b) Having finished the report, she went home. c) The book, written in 1890, is still popular. d) Asked about the scandal, he refused to comment. **3.** a) On the contrary b) On the other hand c) moreover.

## Unit 42 · Englishes of the British Isles

**Goals:** recognise the main accents and dialects of Britain and Ireland, understand variation without imitating it. **Language:** features of regional pronunciation, dialect grammar and vocabulary, attitudes to accent.

![](u42.png)

### Text

A visitor who has learnt English from textbooks may be forgiven for thinking, on arriving in Newcastle, that the locals are speaking another language. They are not. They are speaking Geordie, one of dozens of accents packed into a country about a quarter the size of Spain.

The model used in this book, Standard Southern British English, is spoken natively by a small minority. It carries no geographical label but a strong social one. Most Britons speak with an accent that tells a listener, within seconds, roughly where they grew up.

Broadly speaking, the great divide runs between north and south. Southerners say *bath* with the long vowel of *car*; northerners use the short vowel of *cat*. In the north, *cup* and *put* have the same vowel. Scots and many Irish speakers pronounce every written *r*, whereas most of England does not.

None of these varieties is incorrect. An accent is not a mistake but an address. The learner's task is not to imitate them all, but to be surprised by none.

### Language

**1. The main accent areas**

| Variety | Where | Features to recognise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SSBE / modern RP | south-east; the media | non-rhotic; long /ɑː/ in *bath, grass, dance* |
| Cockney / Estuary | London and around | glottal stop for /t/ (*bu'er*); *th* → /f/, /v/ (*fink, bruvver*); dropped *h* |
| Northern English | Manchester, Leeds, Yorkshire | short /a/ in *bath*; *cup* = /kʊp/; *the* reduced to *t'* in Yorkshire |
| Scouse | Liverpool | /k/ like Spanish «j» (*back* → «baj»); rising tune |
| Geordie | Newcastle | *town* → «toon»; *pet, canny, howay* |
| Scottish | Scotland | rhotic, tapped *r*; *loch* with «j»; no length contrast in *pull / pool* |
| Welsh English | Wales | musical intonation; clear vowels |
| Irish English | Ireland | rhotic; *th* → /t/, /d/ (*tree* for *three*); *after* perfect |

**2. Dialect grammar you will hear**

| Form | Where | Standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *I were, we was* | north, London | I was, we were |
| *I ain't; I didn't see nothing* | widespread | I'm not / haven't; I didn't see anything |
| *them books* | widespread | those books |
| *youse, yous* | Liverpool, Scotland, Ireland | you (plural) |
| *I'm after eating* | Ireland | I've just eaten |
| *Give it me* | north | Give it to me |
| *innit?* | London | isn't it? (used as a universal tag) |

These are regular features of real dialects, not errors, but they are non-standard: understand them and do not write them.

**3. Dialect vocabulary**

*aye* (yes: Scotland, north) · *wee* (small) · *lass / lad* (girl / boy) · *bairn* (child: Scotland, north-east) · *ta* (thanks) · *nowt / owt* (nothing / anything: Yorkshire) · *grand* (fine: Ireland, north) · *mate, love, pet, duck, hen* (friendly forms of address)

**4. Attitudes**

Accent is still linked to class in Britain. RP was once required at the BBC; today regional accents are normal in broadcasting, and a very «posh» accent can be a disadvantage. The trend among young people in the south-east is towards a mixed urban variety.

### Watch out!

- *Accent* is only pronunciation. *Dialect* includes grammar and words. Everybody has an accent, including speakers of the standard.
- Do not try to copy a regional accent you have heard for a week. A consistent, clear, neutral accent is respected everywhere.
- *England*, *Britain* and *the UK* are not synonyms. England is one of four nations of the United Kingdom; Great Britain is the island of England, Scotland and Wales. Calling a Scot «English» is a serious mistake.
- *Ireland* (the Republic) is not part of the UK. Northern Ireland is.
- Strangers in the north may call you *love* or *pet*. It is friendliness, not flirtation.

## Unit 43 · Academic and professional English

**Goals:** write essays, reports and proposals; present data; give a structured presentation. **Language:** impersonal structures, describing trends, citing sources, signposting.

![](u43.png)

### Text

**Summary.** This report examines the take-up of remote working among small firms in the north of England between 2019 and 2024. It is based on a survey of 412 employers and on interviews conducted with a sample of thirty.

**Findings.** The proportion of firms offering some form of remote work rose dramatically, from 11 per cent in 2019 to a peak of 64 per cent in 2021. It then fell steadily before levelling off at around 40 per cent. As can be seen in Figure 2, the decline was most marked in manufacturing, whereas the figure for professional services remained broadly stable.

It is widely assumed that remote working reduces productivity. The data do not support this view. Of the employers surveyed, roughly two thirds reported no change, and a further fifth reported an improvement. It should be noted, however, that these figures are self-reported and should therefore be treated with caution.

**Recommendations.** It is recommended that further research be carried out with a larger sample. In the light of the above, there would appear to be a strong case for retaining hybrid arrangements.

### Language

**1. Impersonal style**

| Personal | Impersonal |
| --- | --- |
| I think that… | It could be argued that… / It seems that… |
| People think… | It is widely assumed that… |
| You can see in Figure 2… | As can be seen in Figure 2… |
| We did interviews. | Interviews were conducted. |
| I recommend more research. | It is recommended that further research be carried out. |

*There* + verb: *There **appears to be** a strong case. · There **has been** a sharp fall.*

**2. Describing trends**

| Verb + adverb | Adjective + noun |
| --- | --- |
| Sales **rose sharply**. | There was **a sharp rise** in sales. |
| The rate **fell steadily**. | There was **a steady fall** in the rate. |
| Prices **increased slightly**. | There was **a slight increase** in prices. |

Prepositions: *rose **from** 11 **to** 64 per cent · rose **by** 53 points · a rise **of** 5 per cent · a fall **in** demand · stood **at** 40 per cent · peaked **at***

**3. Numbers and proportions**

*a third · two thirds · a quarter · a fifth · half · the vast majority · roughly / approximately / just over / just under / well over · one in five · twice as many · a tenfold increase*

**4. Citing and reporting**

*According to Smith (2021)… · As Smith points out / argues / claims / suggests… · Smith's study shows / indicates / demonstrates that… · It has been shown that…*

The verb shows your position: *shows* (you accept it), *claims* (you are doubtful), *suggests* (it is tentative).

**5. Signposting in a presentation**

*I'd like to begin by… · I'll be looking at three areas. · Let's move on to… · As I mentioned earlier… · To put it another way… · This brings me to my final point. · To sum up… · I'd be happy to take any questions.*

### Watch out!

- *Data* is treated as plural in careful academic writing (*the data **show***) and as singular in everyday use.
- «Un aumento **de** precios» = *an increase **in** prices*. «Un aumento **del** 5 %» = *an increase **of** 5 per cent*.
- British English writes *per cent* as two words; *percentage* is the noun: *a high **percentage***, not «a high per cent».
- The decimal point is a point and the thousands separator is a comma: *3.5* (three point five), *1,500*. A British *billion* is now a thousand million, as in the United States.
- *Assume* = suponer; *presume* = dar por hecho; «asumir una responsabilidad» = *take on* or *accept*.
- *Research* is uncountable: *a piece of research*, *a lot of research*, not «researches».
- «Realizar una encuesta» = ***carry out / conduct** a survey*, not «realise».

## Unit 44 · Media and argument

**Goals:** build and evaluate an argument, debate, write a discursive essay and a review. **Language:** conceding and counter-arguing, evaluative language, collocation, rhetorical devices.

![](u44.png)

### Text

It is often claimed that social media has made us better informed. On the face of it, the argument is persuasive. Never before have so many people had access to so much information. Granted, anyone with a phone can now report on events as they happen, and admittedly this has exposed abuses that would once have gone unnoticed.

Yet access is not the same as understanding. While it is true that we read more headlines than ever, it does not follow that we read more carefully. The platforms are designed to hold attention, not to reward accuracy, and outrage travels faster than correction.

Some would argue that readers have only themselves to blame. This is to miss the point. Individuals can hardly be expected to verify every claim; that is precisely what editors were for.

None of this is to suggest that we should turn back the clock. The question is not whether to use these tools but who is accountable for them. Until that is settled, "better informed" will remain, at best, a half-truth.

**4. Rhetorical devices**

- *The rule of three:* "accurate, thorough and unreadable."
- *Antithesis:* "designed to hold attention, not to reward accuracy."
- *The rhetorical question:* "Who is accountable?"
- *Parallelism:* "The question is not whether… but who…"

**5. Reviews**

*It is set in… · It tells the story of… · The plot revolves around… · a gripping / moving / thought-provoking / overrated film · The acting is superb / wooden. · It is well worth seeing. · It fails to live up to expectations.*

### Watch out!

- *Strong* coffee, *heavy* rain, *a heavy smoker*, *a serious illness*, *a big mistake*. Spanish «fuerte» and «grande» do not transfer: not «strong rain».
- ***Make** a mistake / a decision / an effort / progress*; ***do** research / damage / business / your best*.
- *Critic* is a person; *criticism* is the comment; *a review* is «una crítica» of a film or book.
- *Argue* means to give reasons, or to quarrel. «Discutir» in the sense of talking something over is *discuss*, with no preposition: *We discussed the plan*, not «discussed about».
- *Polemic* is rare; say *controversial* for «polémico».
