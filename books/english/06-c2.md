# Level C2 · Mastery

There is no new grammar at C2. What remains is range: the history that explains the oddities of English, the varieties spoken around the world, the language of literature and of specialists, humour, and the ability to carry meaning from one language and one register to another.

## Unit 45 · World Englishes

**Goals:** understand English as a global language, recognise the main international varieties, use English as a lingua franca. **Language:** British and American differences in full, features of other varieties, accommodation strategies.

![](u45.png)

### Text

English belongs to nobody, which is another way of saying that it belongs to everybody. For every native speaker there are now at least three people who use it as a second or foreign language, and most conversations in English take place with no native speaker in the room.

The linguist Braj Kachru pictured this as three circles. In the inner circle are the countries where English is the mother tongue: Britain, Ireland, the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. In the outer circle are those, such as India, Nigeria and Singapore, where it took root through empire and now has an official role and a local character. Beyond lies the expanding circle, Spain included, where it is learnt as a foreign language.

The model in this book is British because a learner needs one consistent point of reference, and because Britain is the nearest shore. But a European who uses English with a Dutch engineer, a Polish client and a Greek landlord is speaking a variety of their own. In that setting, clarity is worth more than idiom, and the speaker who adapts is more skilful than the one who merely sounds native.

**3. Outer-circle varieties**

Indian English: *I am knowing him*; *prepone* (the opposite of *postpone*); *lakh* (100,000); the tag *isn't it?* or *no?* for all persons. Nigerian and Singaporean English have their own particles and rhythms. These are established national standards, not learners' errors.

**4. English as a lingua franca**

What helps international understanding:

| Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| clear consonants and vowel length | swallowing word endings |
| stressing the key word | heavy use of weak forms at speed |
| plain vocabulary | local idioms (*it's not my cup of tea*) |

### Watch out!

- Choose one standard for spelling and keep to it in a document. Mixing *colour* and *center* looks careless.
- *Pants* are «pantalones» in America and «calzoncillos» in Britain. *Rubber* is «goma de borrar» in Britain and «preservativo» in America. *First floor* is not the same floor.
- A date written 3/5 is 3 May in Europe and March 5 in the United States. Write the month as a word.
- In Britain a *public school* is an expensive private school. A state school is a *state school*.
- Being understood by other non-natives is a skill of its own. Native speakers are often the hardest people to follow in an international meeting.

## Unit 46 · The story of English

**Goals:** understand why English is the way it is: its mixed vocabulary, its spelling and its irregularities. **Language:** the history of the language, word origins and doublets, word formation.

![](u46.png)

### Text

English began as the speech of Germanic settlers, the Angles, Saxons and Jutes, who crossed the North Sea in the fifth century. Their language, Old English, would be unintelligible to us now, yet it left the core of the modern tongue: *house, bread, water, mother, love, be, have, the*.

Two invasions transformed it. From the late eighth century the Vikings settled the north and east, and their Old Norse gave English *sky, egg, knife, take* and even the pronoun *they*. Then, in 1066, William of Normandy conquered England, and for three centuries French was the language of power. The peasant looked after the *cow*, the *pig* and the *sheep*, all Germanic words; the lord ate *beef*, *pork* and *mutton*, all French.

The Renaissance brought a flood of Latin and Greek. Printing arrived in 1476 and began to fix the spelling, at the very moment when the pronunciation of the long vowels was shifting. That accident is why English is spelt as it sounded six hundred years ago.

The result is a language with three words for most ideas: a plain Germanic one, a polished French one and a learned Latin one. One can *ask*, *question* or *interrogate*; a king may be *kingly*, *royal* or *regal*.

**2. Why the spelling is so odd**

- **The Great Vowel Shift** (c. 1400–1700): long vowels moved up in the mouth. *Name* was once pronounced like «na-me», *time* like «ti-me», *house* like «hus». The spelling stayed behind.
- **Silent letters** were once pronounced: the *k* in *knight*, the *gh* in *night* (like Spanish «j»), the *l* in *walk*.
- **Scholars added letters** to show Latin origins: *debt* (Latin *debitum*), *doubt*, *island*, *receipt*.
- **Borrowed words keep their spelling**: *ballet, psychology, pizza, tortilla*.

**3. Why the grammar is so simple, and the verbs so irregular**

Contact between Old English and Old Norse wore away the noun and adjective endings, so word order took over their job. The strong verbs (*sing–sang–sung*) are Germanic survivals; the most common words resist change longest.

### Watch out!

- The Latin half of English makes reading easy for Spanish speakers, and makes speech too formal. Germanic words carry everyday life; learn them in pairs.
- Phrasal verbs are the Germanic layer at work: *give up* (surrender), *put out* (extinguish), *come back* (return).
- Because of the vowel shift, the English names of the vowel letters do not match their European values: A /eɪ/, E /iː/, I /aɪ/.
- A doublet is not a synonym. *A royal visit* is fine, but «a kingly visit» is not; *freedom of speech*, but *the Statue of Liberty*.
- Shakespeare's *thou* and *thee* were the familiar «tú»; *you* was the polite and plural form, which then took over completely.

## Unit 47 · Literary English

**Goals:** read literature in English with understanding and pleasure, recognise literary devices and older forms. **Language:** figurative language, sound and rhythm, narrative technique, archaic grammar.

![](u47.png)

### Text

Open a novel written two centuries ago and the first surprise is how little has changed. The sentences are longer and the manners stiffer, but the language is ours. Go back four centuries, to the plays of Shakespeare's time, and the ground begins to move: familiar words mean unfamiliar things, verbs end in *-eth*, and a character may say *thou* to a servant and *you* to a king.

Literary English is not a separate language. It is the ordinary language under pressure. The poet wants a word to do three jobs at once: to mean something, to sound like something, and to remind us of something else. A novelist may tell a story through the eyes of a narrator who does not understand it, and trust the reader to see what the teller cannot.

The reader's task is to slow down. In everyday reading we look through the words to the message. In literature the words are the message. Ask not only what is said, but who is saying it, to whom, and why in these words rather than any others. If a line resists you, read it aloud. English verse was written for the ear, and the ear often understands before the mind.

**2. Sound**

*Alliteration* repeats initial consonants: *the slow, sad sea*. *Assonance* repeats vowels: *the deep green sleep of trees*. *Onomatopoeia* imitates a sound: *buzz, crack, murmur, hiss*.

The basic rhythm of English verse is the **iambic** foot: a weak syllable followed by a strong one. Five of them make an iambic pentameter, the line of Shakespeare's plays and sonnets:

*da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM* — *The clock had stopped; the house was still and cold.*

It is close to the natural rhythm of English speech, which is why it sounds like heightened talk and not like singing.

**5. False friends across time**

In older texts *nice* could mean «foolish» or «precise», *silly* «innocent», *presently* «immediately», *to want* «to lack», *awful* «inspiring awe», and *gay* «cheerful».

### Watch out!

- *Wherefore* means «por qué», not «dónde». A famous line about a lover's name asks why he is who he is, not where he is.
- *Thou* is singular and familiar, like «tú». It is not a more solemn form of *you*; it feels solemn only because it survives in prayers.
- A *novel* is long fiction. «Novela corta» is *a novella*; «relato» is *a short story*. *Verse* is poetry in general or a line of it; «verso» as a single line is *a line*.
- *Argument* in old books may mean «summary of the plot».
- Read the punctuation in verse, not the line endings: the sense often runs over from one line into the next.

## Unit 48 · Plain English and specialist languages

**Goals:** read legal, medical, financial and bureaucratic texts; rewrite jargon in plain language. **Language:** features of legal and official English, medical and business registers, plain-English principles.

![](u48.png)

### Text

Consider the following clause from a tenancy agreement:

*"The Tenant shall not, without the prior written consent of the Landlord, such consent not to be unreasonably withheld, assign, sublet or otherwise part with possession of the Premises or any part thereof."*

It means: *You must ask the landlord in writing before you let anyone else live here. The landlord cannot refuse without a good reason.*

The original is forty words long and contains nothing a lawyer would call unclear. Every phrase has been tested in court, and the lawyer who replaces *thereof* with *of it* takes a risk for no reward. That is how specialist languages survive: they are precise for insiders and opaque to everyone else.

Since the 1970s a movement for plain English has argued that people have a right to understand the documents that govern their lives. Its rules are simple. Use short sentences. Prefer the active voice. Say *you* and *we*. Choose the everyday word. And test the text on the people who will have to read it.

- These are false friends with legal consequences: *sentence* (condena, no «sentencia» en general), *sanction* (can mean to approve as well as to punish), *process* (not «un proceso judicial», which is *proceedings* or *a trial*), *magistrate* (a lower-court judge, often not a lawyer), *notary* (not the powerful «notario» of Spain).
- *Shall* in a contract is an obligation, not a future.
- English law is common law, based on precedent. Many Spanish legal concepts have no exact equivalent, and a term may need explaining, not translating.
- Plain English is not simplified English. It is harder to write than jargon.
- *Casualty* means «víctima» or the hospital emergency department, not «casualidad» (*coincidence, chance*).

## Unit 49 · Humour, irony and understatement

**Goals:** understand British humour, recognise irony and sarcasm, use idioms and wordplay. **Language:** irony and understatement, puns, idioms and proverbs, euphemism, self-deprecation.

![](u49.png)

### Text

A foreigner who takes the British at their word will be lost within a day. "We must have lunch sometime" is not an invitation. "That's a very brave proposal" means it is mad. "I'm sure it's my fault" is the opening shot of a complaint. And when, on a morning of horizontal rain, a neighbour remarks that it is "not the best of days", he is exercising a national art.

British humour runs on the gap between what is said and what is meant. Understatement shrinks disasters to inconveniences. Irony praises what it despises. Self-deprecation gets the insult in first, so that nobody else can. The voice stays level and the face straight; to signal the joke would spoil it.

For the learner this is the last and highest wall. Grammar can be studied and vocabulary memorised, but irony must be heard. The clue is usually a mismatch: words too mild for the situation, or too polite for the speaker's obvious mood. When in doubt, assume that an Englishman who says he is "a bit annoyed" is furious, and that one who calls your work "interesting" has not finished his sentence.

**2. Irony, sarcasm and understatement**

*Irony* says the opposite of what is meant: *"Lovely weather!"* (in a storm). *Sarcasm* is irony used to wound: *"Oh, brilliant. Well done."* *Understatement* (litotes) says less: *"It's not exactly cheap." · "He's no fool." · "I've had better days."*

**3. Puns and wordplay**

English has thousands of words that sound alike, so it puns constantly, above all in newspaper headlines and shop names. A fishmonger's called *The Codfather*; a hairdresser's called *Curl Up and Dye*. The pun depends on *homophones* (*die / dye*, *sole / soul*, *flour / flower*) or on words with two meanings.

### Watch out!

- Idioms cannot be translated word for word, and they cannot be changed: *it's raining cats and dogs*, not «dogs and cats». Half a proverb is enough: *Well, when in Rome…*
- An idiom used slightly wrong is worse than plain language. Recognise many, use few.
- *Sorry* is said to people who bump into you, and it rarely means regret. *Cheers* means «salud», «gracias» and «adiós».
- Sarcasm from a learner is risky: without native intonation it is taken literally.
- *Humour* (BrE) is spelt *humor* in American English, but *humorous* in both.
- The British tease people they like. Banter is a sign of acceptance, and the correct reply is another joke, not an explanation.

## Unit 50 · Mastery: style and mediation

**Goals:** edit your own writing to a professional standard, mediate between languages and registers, go on learning alone. **Language:** style and concision, punctuation, translation from Spanish, summarising and mediating.

![](u50.png)

### Text

To know a language well is to be able to make it disappear. The reader of a good text does not notice the words; the listener to a good interpreter forgets that there are two languages in the room.

This is harder than it looks, and hardest of all between languages as close as Spanish and English appear to be. Spanish prose likes long sentences, abstract nouns and a clause held back until the end. English prefers a short sentence with a strong verb and the main point first. A Spanish paragraph translated faithfully, word by word, produces English that is grammatical, elegant in its way, and plainly foreign.

Mediation asks for more than translation. It means reading a contract and telling a friend what she is signing; listening to a doctor and explaining to a relative what will happen; summarising a meeting for those who were not there. In each case you decide what matters, what can be left out, and how this particular listener needs to hear it.

There is no final unit in a language. There is only the next book, the next conversation and the next word you did not know. If this course has done its work, you no longer need it. Go and read something difficult.

### Language

**1. Six rules of style**

| Rule | Wordy | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cut empty words | due to the fact that | because |
| Prefer verbs to nouns | carry out an investigation into | investigate |
| Prefer the concrete | adverse weather conditions | rain |
| Put the point first | After much thought and for many reasons, I resign. | I resign. My reasons are these. |
| One idea, one sentence | | |
| Read it aloud | | |

**2. Punctuation that Spanish speakers get wrong**

| Mark | English rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comma | never between two full sentences (the «comma splice») | wrong: *It was late, we went home.* → right: *It was late, so we went home.* |
| Comma | not before *that* | *He said that he was tired.* |
| Semicolon | joins two related sentences | *It was late; we went home.* |
| Colon | introduces an explanation or list; no capital after it | *There is one rule: read.* |
| Apostrophe | possession and contraction, never plurals | *the dog's bowl; it's raining; two dogs* |
| Question and exclamation marks | only at the end | *How are you?* |
| Quotation marks | ' ' or " ", never « » | *"Hello," she said.* |
| Capitals | days, months, languages, nationalities | *on Monday in May; a Spanish class* |
| Dash | informal aside | *I'll come – if I can.* |

*its* (possessive) / *it's* (it is) · *whose* / *who's* · *their* / *there* / *they're* · *your* / *you're*

**3. From Spanish to English**

| Spanish habit | English preference | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| long sentences with subordination | shorter sentences | split at «y», «que», «donde» |
| noun style | verb style | *la realización del estudio* → *when we did the study* |
| passive with «se» | passive, or *you / we / people* | *Se dice* → *People say / It is said* |
| article with abstract nouns | no article | *La vida es breve* → *Life is short* |
| subject omitted or after the verb | subject first | *Llegó el tren* → *The train arrived* |
| double negative | single negative | *No vi a nadie* → *I didn't see anyone* |
| formal connectors (*asimismo*, *por otra parte*) | fewer, plainer connectors | *also*, *but* |
| adjective after noun, several of them | fixed order before the noun | *a lovely little old stone house* |

The order of adjectives: opinion – size – age – shape – colour – origin – material – purpose + noun.

**4. Summarising and mediating**

1. Read or listen for the gist first: who, what, why.
2. Select: keep the decisions, numbers and dates; drop examples and repetition.
3. Adapt to the listener: a lawyer's text for a friend becomes *you* and *must*.
4. Stay neutral: report, do not comment. Signal when you are unsure: *As far as I can tell… · What she seems to be saying is…*
5. Check: *Does that make sense? · Shall I go over that again?*

**5. How to keep learning**

Read widely and above your level. Keep a notebook of collocations, not single words. Listen to unscripted speech. Write something every week and have it corrected. Notice what native writers do that you would not have done, and steal it.

### Watch out!

- The comma splice is the commonest punctuation error in advanced Spanish speakers' English. Two sentences need a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction.
- Do not translate «¡» and «¿»; do not use «» as quotation marks; do not write days, months or languages without capitals.
- «No vi nada» = *I saw **nothing*** or *I didn't see **anything***. Two negatives make a non-standard sentence.
- *Translate* is for writing; *interpret* is for speech. A *translator* is not an *interpreter*.
- *Resume* means «reanudar». «Resumir» is *summarise*. A *résumé* is an American CV.
- Elegance in English is brevity. If you can cut a word, cut it.
